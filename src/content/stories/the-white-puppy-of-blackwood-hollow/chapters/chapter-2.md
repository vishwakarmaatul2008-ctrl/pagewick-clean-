# Chapter 2 — The Whispers in the House

Aarav's breath caught in his throat, frozen into a silent scream.

The voice coming from the tiny, malformed mouth wasn't a growl or a trick of the wind. It was his mother's voice—exact down to the gentle pitch, the warm cadence, the slight rasp she got when she was tired. But coming out of a creature whose spine was currently folding into unnatural geometric angles beneath the bed frame, it was pure horror.

"Aarav... come down on the floor with me," it repeated. The small white skull twitched, its neck snapping another inch to the side with a dry, wooden pop.

Aarav scrambled backward on his mattress, his back slamming hard against the headboard. He fumbled frantically for the bedside lamp, his shaking hand finally striking the switch.

Warm yellow light flooded the bedroom.

The moment the light hit the floor, the scratching stopped. Aarav leaned over the edge of the mattress, his chest heaving, his phone flashlight still burning in his grip.

Underneath the bed, the white puppy was lying down peacefully on the blanket. It looked entirely normal again—a tiny, innocent white ball of fur. Its eyes were soft, wet, and blinking against the light. There were no needle teeth. Its jaw was closed.

For three long minutes, Aarav didn't move. The silence in the room was suffocating. He watched the puppy's ribs rise and fall, but he noticed something horrifying that he hadn't seen in the dark: it wasn't breathing. Its chest moved up and down in a rigid, mechanical rhythm, like a clockwork toy pretending to draw air.

Aarav didn't sleep for the rest of the night. He sat pressed against the corner of his wall, clutching a wooden baseball bat, staring at the gap beneath his bed until the sky outside turned a sickly, bruised gray.

By morning, the terror hadn't vanished—it had infected the entire house.

When Aarav dragged himself downstairs for breakfast, the family's seven-year-old Golden Retriever, Bruno, was refusing to leave the laundry room. Bruno was whining hysterically, pressing his face into the corner where the dryer met the wall, his coat wet with panic sweat. He wouldn't touch his food, and every time Aarav walked into the kitchen, Bruno let out a low, terrified whimper.

Aarav's mother was standing by the stove, rubbing her temples. "I don't know what's wrong with the air in this house today," she murmured, her voice sounding unnervingly close to the voice under the bed last night. "My head has been pounding since 3:00 AM. It feels... heavy. Like someone is standing right behind me."

Aarav's heart hammered against his ribs. He had tried to keep the white puppy hidden in his room, but at 10:00 AM, his father went upstairs to grab a jacket—and found it.

"Aarav! Get up here now!" his father called out.

Aarav ran up the stairs. His father was standing in the doorway of Aarav's bedroom, staring inside with a look of intense revulsion.

The white puppy wasn't on the bed or under it. It was sitting directly in the middle of the room, facing the blank plaster wall. It wasn't moving. It wasn't sniffing around. It was just staring into the empty corner, completely unblinking.

"Where did you get this?" his father asked, his tone unusually harsh.

"We... we found it near the park yesterday, Dad. In the rain," Aarav stammered, keeping a five-foot distance from the creature. "I was going to take it to the shelter today."

"Get it out of this house," his father said coldly. He wasn't usually a strict man, but his hands were trembling as he pointed toward the floor. "I don't care where you put it. Take it back to the park. Now. There is something... fundamentally wrong with that animal."

His mother walked up the stairs, holding her head, but stopped dead in her tracks three steps from the landing. She looked at the white puppy, her skin turning an ash-gray color.

"Aarav," she whispered, her voice shaking violently. "Why is it wearing your sister's hair ribbon?"

Aarav froze.

Tucked behind the puppy's left ear was a frayed pink ribbon—the exact ribbon Maya had been wearing in the park yesterday. Maya was currently downstairs, asleep in her room.

The puppy slowly rotated its head away from the blank wall. It didn't turn its body. It just twisted its neck 180 degrees until its coal-black eyes were locked directly onto Aarav's mother.

It opened its mouth, just a sliver. A faint, wet whisper trickled out—so quiet only Aarav and his mother could hear it:

"Mommy... my head hurts too."

His mother gasped, dropping to her knees on the stairs, clutching her throat as if the air had been sucked out of her lungs.

"Get it out!" his father roared, snatching an empty laundry basket and slamming it over the puppy.

Through the white plastic mesh of the basket, the puppy didn't struggle. It didn't cry out. It just lay completely flat, its legs tucking inside its torso like a folding chair, its face pressed flat against the carpet.

Ten minutes later, Aarav was running down the muddy trail toward the neighborhood park, carrying the covered laundry basket at arm's length. The sun was hidden behind dense, suffocating clouds, and the Appalachian forest surrounding the park seemed to leaning forward, watching him.

He reached the rusty boundary fence near the old Miller house. With a burst of frantic energy, Aarav tipped the basket over, dumping the white creature onto the wet grass near the treeline.

The puppy landed softly. It stood up on its four short legs, entirely unbothered.

"Stay away from us," Aarav sobbed, his voice breaking as he backed away toward the sidewalk. "Stay away from my house!"

The puppy didn't move. It just stood at the edge of the dark, towering pine trees, watching Aarav with those hollow, lightless eyes.

As Aarav turned and ran back toward his neighborhood, he didn't hear a dog bark. He heard the sound of a little girl giggling from deep within the woods—a high, clear laugh that sounded identically like his sister Maya.
