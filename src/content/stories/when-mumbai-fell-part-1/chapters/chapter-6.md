# Chapter 6: The Way Out

"Keep moving," Aarav said.

It was the only instruction he could offer.

The six friends hurried down the side road, leaving the shuttered building behind. They avoided the main street, where cars had stopped at strange angles and people were running between vehicles.

Every few seconds, a distant scream cut through the traffic noise.

Mumbai was still moving, but it no longer seemed to know where it was going.

A man abandoned his motorcycle in the middle of the road and ran toward a shop. A woman dragged a suitcase behind her while shouting into her phone. Two strangers argued over a car door until a scream from the next junction sent them running in opposite directions.

The friends kept their heads down.

"How far can we go on foot?" Dev asked.

"Depends where we're going," Sana replied.

"Excellent. We have successfully identified the problem."

"Dev," Meera warned.

"I'm coping."

Aarav checked his phone again.

No signal.

He tried his mother once more. The call failed before it could connect.

He stared at the screen, feeling a wave of guilt.

He should have called her earlier. He should have told her where he was going. He should have done a hundred ordinary things that had seemed unnecessary that morning.

Now all those things felt urgent.

Sana noticed him falling behind.

"Any luck?"

He shook his head.

"We'll keep trying," she said. "We can't assume something has happened just because the network is down."

He nodded and put the phone away.

They reached a small intersection. A few shops had already closed their shutters. Across the road, smoke rose from a vehicle whose front end had been crushed against a divider.

A group of people ran past them.

One shouted, "Don't go toward the station!"

Another yelled something about an attack near a hospital.

The warnings contradicted each other. Nobody knew which roads were safe or where the danger had started.

Kabir looked down the main road.

"We should find a vehicle."

"And then drive where?" Sana asked. "The roads are blocked. We don't know which routes are clear."

"We can't stay here."

"I didn't say we should."

Rishi raised his phone.

"My brother said to find somewhere secure. Maybe we can reach his building."

"How far?" Aarav asked.

"Normally? Maybe twenty-five minutes by car."

"Normally isn't useful right now."

Rishi's face tightened.

"I know that."

Aarav immediately regretted his tone.

"Sorry. I just mean we need to know whether it's possible."

Rishi looked at the road again.

"He's safe for now. If we can reach him, at least we'll have a place to stay."

"Then let's check the route before we commit," Sana said.

They moved toward a quieter lane, keeping away from the main junction. The buildings were taller here, their windows reflecting a grey sky. Most shops were shut, but a small convenience store remained open.

Aarav looked through its glass door.

Shelves were still stocked with bottled water, packaged snacks, and basic supplies. The shopkeeper stood behind the counter, watching the street nervously.

"We need water," Sana said.

"And food," Dev added. "Preferably food that doesn't require me to become a chef during the apocalypse."

They entered cautiously.

The shopkeeper looked at them.

"Buy what you need and leave. I'm closing soon."

They gathered bottled water, packets of biscuits, and a few other packaged foods. Aarav paid with his phone, relieved when the transaction went through.

As they packed the supplies, a loud crash sounded outside.

Everyone turned.

A man had fallen against the shop's window. He scrambled to his feet, looking behind him.

Two figures came around the corner.

The man ran.

One of the figures followed, stumbling into the road before catching itself against a parked scooter.

The shopkeeper hurried to the entrance and pulled the shutter down halfway.

"Out!" he shouted. "Go out the back!"

"There is a back?" Dev asked.

The shopkeeper pointed toward a narrow doorway beside the storage shelves.

The friends hurried through it into a small service lane.

The shutter crashed closed behind them.

They stood in the alley, clutching their supplies.

For a few seconds, nobody spoke.

Then Kabir let out a shaky laugh.

"We bought biscuits during the end of the world."

"You're welcome," Meera said. "When society collapses, at least you'll have something to complain about while chewing."

They continued down the lane.

At the end stood another commercial building, its entrance protected by a metal gate. Several people were already inside, arguing with a guard who refused to let anyone else through.

Rishi stepped forward.

"Please. We just need somewhere safe for a while."

The guard shook his head.

"The building is closing. I've been told not to admit anyone else."

"There are people being attacked outside!"

"I know!"

The guard's voice cracked. He looked frightened, not cruel.

"If I open this gate, everyone will rush in. I can't control it. Please go."

A pounding sound came from the far end of the road.

The guard turned.

A group of infected people had appeared near the intersection. They were moving toward the noise of the crowd.

The guard pulled the gate shut and locked it.

Rishi stared at him.

"Seriously?"

Aarav grabbed his shoulder.

"Come on."

They ran back into the lane.

The sound of the infected grew louder behind them. Their shoes slapped against the pavement as they raced between closed shops and parked scooters.

At the next turn, Sana spotted a narrow passage between two buildings.

"Here!"

They squeezed into it.

The passage was barely wide enough for two people to walk side by side. It smelled of damp concrete and rubbish. At the far end, a small metal gate stood partly open.

Kabir pushed it wider.

Beyond it was a courtyard surrounded by apartment buildings.

For the first time in what felt like hours, they found a place where they couldn't immediately see the street.

They hurried inside and pulled the gate shut.

Nobody knew whether it would hold.

Dev sat on the ground, clutching a bottle of water.

"I'm never making fun of survival videos again."

Meera slid down the wall beside him.

"You're absolutely going to make fun of them again."

"Not today."

"Fair."

Sana checked the gate, then the windows overlooking the courtyard.

"We can't stay here forever. We need to figure out which building is occupied, whether anyone will let us in, and how we're going to contact our families."

Aarav looked at his phone.

Still no signal.

He tried his mother's number one last time.

The call failed.

For a moment, he imagined her sitting at home, watching the same frightening news reports, calling him again and again.

He pictured the unanswered calls appearing on her screen.

His throat tightened.

"I should have called her earlier," he whispered.

Rishi sat beside him.

"You couldn't have known this would happen."

"I knew something was wrong at the hospital. I saw the news. I still went to get food."

"We all did," Rishi said. "We all thought it was nothing."

Aarav looked at his friends.

Six people who had joked about surviving a zombie outbreak less than twenty-four hours earlier.

They had laughed about weapons, escape routes, and who would die first.

None of them had understood what survival actually meant.

It meant being afraid to approach an injured person because you didn't know whether they would attack you.

It meant leaving people behind when you couldn't safely help them.

It meant being unable to reach your own family.

It meant making decisions without knowing whether they were right.

And it meant that even after finding a temporary hiding place, the danger didn't disappear.

A distant siren rose above the buildings, then faded.

Somewhere nearby, glass shattered.

Sana stood up.

"We need to make a plan before it gets dark."

Kabir rubbed his face.

"Okay. What do we have?"

"Six people," Aarav said. "Some water and food. Phones that might work again if the network comes back. No vehicle. No confirmed safe location."

"That sounds terrible when you say it out loud," Dev replied.

"It's still what we have."

Meera looked toward the narrow gate.

"And what if this place isn't safe?"

Aarav didn't answer immediately.

Yesterday, he would have had a dozen theories. He would have explained how people should prepare, which mistakes they should avoid, and what he would do if the worst ever happened.

Today, every answer felt smaller than the problem.

"We keep watch," he said finally. "We contact our families whenever we can. We find out what's happening outside without wandering blindly into the streets. Then we decide where to go next."

Sana nodded.

"That's a start."

Rishi looked at the others.

"Nobody goes anywhere alone. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Kabir said.

Dev raised his bottle.

"To the world's worst shopping trip."

Meera almost smiled.

"Don't jinx it. We haven't even found a bathroom."

For a moment, the familiar rhythm returned. The teasing. The sarcasm. The small, ridiculous comments that had always made them feel like themselves.

Then another scream echoed beyond the courtyard wall.

The moment vanished.

They were still together. They were still alive.

But outside, Mumbai was changing faster than any of them could understand.

The outbreak had begun in a crowded mall.

Now it was spreading through streets, hospitals, homes, and every place people had once believed was safe.

The six friends had found a temporary hiding place, but they had no guarantee it would protect them until morning.

And morning was no longer something they could take for granted.
