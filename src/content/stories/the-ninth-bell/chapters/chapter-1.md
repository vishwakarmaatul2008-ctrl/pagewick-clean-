# Chapter 1: The Bell That Shouldn't Ring

Thorne Abbey had eight bells. Everyone knew that. It was carved into the brass plaque bolted beside the clocktower door, worn smooth by two hundred years of nervous first-years running their fingers over the words on the way to chapel: *Eight bells mark the hour. Silence marks the rest.*

Nell Ashworth had heard the eighth bell toll every night at ten since she arrived three weeks ago, same as everyone else in the scholarship wing. She'd learned to sleep through it, the way you learn to sleep through anything that repeats enough times to stop being strange.

She was not asleep the night the ninth bell rang.

It came exactly four seconds after the eighth — low, uneven, half a tone flat, like something forcing its way through a throat that wasn't built to make the sound. Nell sat up in the dark, certain she'd imagined it, until she heard doors opening up and down the corridor. Other people had heard it too.

"That's not possible," she said, to no one, pulling her cardigan around her shoulders as she crossed to the window.

The clocktower stood black against a sky gone the color of a bruise, its four faces lit faintly from within, the way they always were. Nothing about it looked different. But down in the quad, a knot of students in nightclothes had gathered at the base of the tower, all of them looking up, all of them very still.

Nell was down the stairs and across the wet grass before she'd decided to move.

"Did you hear it too?" asked a boy she didn't know, his voice pitched too high for someone trying to sound calm. "The ninth one. I counted. Everyone counted."

"It's a mechanical fault," said a girl in a St. Aldric's prefect sash, though she didn't sound convinced of it either. "The bells are two hundred years old. Things break."

"Things that break don't ring in tune with the other eight," said a third voice, quieter, and Nell turned to find a tall boy standing slightly apart from the group, arms crossed, watching the tower with an expression that wasn't fear so much as recognition — like he'd been waiting for this and was almost relieved it had finally happened.

"You're the archivist's assistant," Nell said. She'd seen him in the library, cataloguing things in the restricted stacks that no one else was allowed to touch. "Idris, right?"

"Idris Vane." He didn't look at her. "You're new. Ashworth. Scholarship."

"Everyone keeps saying that like it explains something."

"It might." He finally glanced down at her, and in the tower's pale light his eyes looked older than the rest of his face. "Do you know what happens after the ninth bell rings, Ashworth?"

"I didn't know a ninth bell existed an hour ago."

"Neither did most of them." He nodded at the crowd, who were already starting to drift back toward their dormitories, laughing too loudly, the way people laugh when they're trying to convince themselves nothing happened. "By morning, half of them will have decided they misheard. The other half will remember, and wish they hadn't."

"Why?"

Idris was quiet for a moment, long enough that Nell thought he wasn't going to answer.

"Because the last time it rang," he said finally, "was ninety-one years ago. And nine days later, a girl named Sabine Holt walked out of her dormitory in the middle of the night and was never seen again. No body. No note. Her window was locked from the inside." He looked back up at the tower. "The abbey called it a runaway. Everyone else called it what it was."

"Which was?"

"I don't know yet." For the first time, something flickered in his composure — not fear exactly, but the careful blankness of someone who'd rehearsed staying calm about this for a very long time. "But the bell only rings the night before it starts choosing. Nine days, Ashworth. That's how long we have before it happens again."

"Before *what* happens again?"

Idris finally turned to face her fully, and whatever he saw in her expression seemed to decide something for him.

"Someone in this school is going to disappear," he said. "The bell already knows who. It rang for them tonight — we just don't know which one of us was standing close enough to hear it meant for them." He started walking toward the archive wing, and after a beat, glanced back when she didn't follow. "You should come. If you want to know why the abbey spent ninety years making sure no one ever counted past eight."

Nell looked up once more at the tower, its four dark faces giving nothing away, and then went after him — telling herself it was curiosity, and trying not to notice the small, cold certainty settling under her ribs that she already knew, somehow, that the countdown had already started, and that she was going to spend the next nine days trying to outrun it.
