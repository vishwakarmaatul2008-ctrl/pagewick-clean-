# Chapter 3: Day Seven

By the seventh day, half the school had decided the whole thing was nonsense, and the other half had stopped sleeping.

Nell fell somewhere in between. She went to lectures. She ate in the great hall under the age-blackened portraits of headmasters long dead. She told herself, in the bright hours of the afternoon, that a two-hundred-year-old building had settled, or a pipe had knocked, or a dozen sleep-deprived teenagers had heard exactly what they expected to hear. And then night came, and she lay awake counting the eighth bell, and the silence after it, waiting.

It was during Thursday's history seminar that she first noticed something wrong with Priya Okonkwo.

Priya sat two rows ahead, a scholarship student like Nell, sharp enough to correct the professor twice a week and usually first to raise her hand. That Thursday she said nothing. She sat with her hands flat on the desk, staring at a single spot on the wall, and when Professor Calloway called her name to answer a question, it took her three tries to hear him.

"Miss Okonkwo. Are you with us?"

"Sorry," Priya said, blinking like she was surfacing from deep water. "Sorry, sir. Could you repeat the question?"

Nell caught up with her after class, in the corridor outside, where the light through the mullioned windows fell in long grey bars across the flagstones.

"You okay? You seemed somewhere else in there."

Priya's smile came a half-second too late. "Fine. Didn't sleep well."

"Nobody's sleeping well this week."

"It's not that." Priya's eyes flicked toward the window, toward the clocktower visible across the quad, and something in her expression made the hair on the back of Nell's neck rise. "I keep dreaming about it. The tower. Every night since — since the bell." She laughed, short and unconvincing. "It's stupid. It's just a dream."

"What happens in it?"

Priya was quiet long enough that Nell thought she wouldn't answer. When she did, her voice had dropped almost to nothing.

"I'm standing at the bottom of the stairs inside the tower, and I can hear something moving at the top, coming down. And I know — I just *know*, the way you know things in dreams — that if I look up before it reaches the bottom, something very bad happens. So I stand there with my eyes on the floor, and I can hear it getting closer, and closer, and just as it's about to reach me—" She stopped. Shook her head. "I wake up. Every night. Same exact point."

Nell found Idris in the library an hour later, hunched over the same ledger from before, and told him everything.

"That's not a coincidence," Idris said immediately, closing the book. "Sabine Holt's roommate reported the same dream in the days before she vanished. It's in the appendix — a footnote, easy to miss. Everyone assumed it was grief talking, retroactive, the roommate reading meaning into an ordinary nightmare after the fact." He looked up at Nell, his expression grim. "But your friend's having it *before* anything's happened. That means it's not memory. It's a warning."

"A warning of what?"

"I think," Idris said slowly, "the bell doesn't choose at random at all. I think it marks someone the moment it rings, and then it spends nine days making sure they're ready — softening them, or wearing them down, however you want to think about it — before whatever comes for them arrives to collect." He pushed the ledger toward her, tapping the same footnote. "Which means if Priya's having that dream, she might already be the one it rang for."

Nell felt the floor tilt slightly beneath her. "We have to tell someone. The Headmaster, her parents, someone—"

"We tried that in 1934. The Headmaster's response is in this book too." Idris's mouth twisted. "He recorded it as *the girl's nerves, exacerbated by examination stress.* Nobody with any power in this school has ever once taken it seriously, Ashworth. If we want to stop this, it's going to have to be us."

Outside, across the quad, the clocktower's four dark faces watched the courtyard the way they always did — patient, unreadable, and, for the first time, Nell thought she understood why the old plaque bothered to specify. *Eight bells mark the hour. Silence marks the rest.* As if someone, once, had known exactly what the silence was hiding, and had tried, in the only way they were allowed, to warn everyone who came after.
