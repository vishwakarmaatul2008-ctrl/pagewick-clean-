# Chapter 3 — The Woman Who Wasn't in the Garden

Naina didn't sleep that night, not really.

She sat on the living room floor with the album closed in front of her, watching the clock, waiting for 3 a.m. the way someone waits for a storm they can see coming.

At 2:58, she opened it.

Page six.

Empty.

She almost laughed with relief.

Then, as she watched — she actually watched it happen this time — the blank cardboard began to change.

Not like a photograph developing in a darkroom.

More like something underneath the page was rising to the surface, the way frost creeps across glass.

An image bled into being.

Naina at nine years old. The garden from page three — except now she recognized it, because the memory was suddenly, sickeningly present in her mind, fully formed, as if it had always been there.

A garden behind a house she didn't know. A swing. A woman standing beside her, hand on her shoulder.

Naina stared at the woman's face and felt the floor tilt beneath her.

She knew that face.

She didn't know that face.

Both things were true at once, and she couldn't hold them together in her mind without her vision swimming.

The woman in the photograph was smiling at the camera the way you smile at someone you love.

Naina had never seen her before in her life.

And yet — somewhere underneath the panic — a warmth. A familiarity. The specific, physical memory of that hand on her shoulder.

She grabbed her phone and called her mother again, too fast, fingers clumsy on the screen.

"Who is this?" she said the moment her mother picked up, not bothering with hello, holding the phone's camera up to the photograph even though she knew her mother couldn't see it yet. She sent the picture instead, hands shaking.

The silence stretched so long Naina thought the call had dropped.

"Mum?"

"Where did you get this?"

"The album. Grandma's album. Who is she?"

"Naina." Her mother's voice had gone thin, stretched over something. "I need you to leave that apartment."

"Tell me who she is."

"I don't know who she is."

"You're lying."

"I'm not — " Her mother stopped herself. Started again, quieter. "I don't recognize her. I've never seen that woman before in my life. But Naina — that's not what frightens me."

"Then what does?"

"I recognize the garden."

Naina looked down at the photograph again. At the swing. At the unfamiliar house behind it.

"That's not possible," her mother said, so quietly Naina almost didn't hear it. "That garden was torn up thirty years ago. Before you were even born."

Neither of them spoke for a long moment.

"I'm coming to get you," her mother said finally. "Don't open that album again. Please."

She hung up before Naina could answer.

Naina sat alone in the dark living room, the album open in her lap, the impossible garden staring up at her.

She thought about what Mrs. Dutta had said.

*He only takes what's already been forgotten.*

She looked at the woman's face again — familiar and foreign in the same breath — and understood, with a horror she couldn't yet name, that the woman hadn't been forgotten.

She had been *replaced.*

Somewhere, in the version of her life that used to be true, this woman had mattered.

And Naina had no memory of losing her at all.

She turned to page seven, half-hoping, half-dreading to see something there already.

It was blank.

But at the very bottom of the page, so faint she almost missed it, something was already beginning to surface.

Not an image yet.

Just a shape.

Waiting.
