# Chapter 7 — What Naina Chose to Keep

"Think about what you're saying," her mother said, gripping her arm. "You won't remember any of this. You won't remember me telling you I loved you. You won't remember tonight at all."

"I know."

"Naina — "

"You'll remember it," Naina said, turning to face her fully. "You said it yourself. That part has to stay somewhere. Let it stay with you instead of me."

Her mother was crying too hard to answer.

The shape in the corner waited, patient as stone.

"Do it," Naina said to it. "Take the debt. All of it. Tonight."

"You understand there is no undoing this," the shape said. "Not tomorrow. Not in sixty years, when you are old yourself, and wondering why an album sits on your shelf that you have no memory of ever opening."

"I understand."

"Then it is done," the shape said, and the room seemed to exhale.

Naina's mother watched, frozen, as her daughter's face went suddenly, briefly slack — the particular blankness of someone standing in a room they no longer recognize, holding the hand of someone they no longer have a name for.

Then Naina blinked, and focused, and looked at her mother with polite, careful confusion — the look you give a stranger who has grabbed your hand on the street.

"I'm sorry," Naina said. "Do I know you?"

Her mother made a sound like something breaking, and pulled her into an embrace instead of answering, holding on so tightly that Naina — bewildered, but not frightened, because there was nothing in this woman's arms that felt like danger — simply let herself be held.

On the coffee table, the album lay open to a page that was, once again, and permanently, blank.

Pages one through six had gone with it. Not torn out. Simply empty, as if they had never held anything at all.

The shape in the corner was gone.

In the weeks that followed, Naina's mother helped her pack up her grandmother's apartment, and never once mentioned an album, a photograph, a name, or a debt. She answered Naina's occasional questions about her childhood with careful, ordinary stories — sanded smooth of anything that might reopen a door neither of them could afford to open again.

Naina noticed, sometimes, a strange gap where a memory should be. A birthday she couldn't quite picture. A woman's face that surfaced in dreams and dissolved the moment she woke, leaving only the ghost of a hand on her shoulder.

She told herself it didn't matter.

Grief did strange things to memory. Everyone said so.

She kept the album anyway, on the bottom shelf of her own bookcase now, empty and closed, and never once felt the urge to open it.

Her mother visited every Sunday.

She always arrived early, and she always, without fail, held Naina's hand a little longer than the greeting required — as if checking, every single week, that it was still there to hold.

Naina never asked why.

Some things, she'd learned without knowing how she'd learned them, were better left exactly where they were.

On the shelf behind her, in the dark, the album's final page sat blank and waiting.

It would be a very long time before anyone opened it again.

**The End.**
