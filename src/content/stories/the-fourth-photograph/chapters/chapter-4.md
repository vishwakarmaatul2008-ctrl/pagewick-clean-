# Chapter 4 — The Address Behind the Glass

Naina didn't wait for her mother.

She couldn't sit still in that apartment, not with page seven quietly filling itself in on the table behind her, so she did the only thing that felt like action instead of waiting: she pulled the photographs out of their sleeves, one at a time, looking for anything written on the back.

The first three were blank.

The fourth had nothing either.

The fifth — the birthday photograph — had a faint pencil mark on the back that she'd missed before. A smudge that resolved, under the kitchen light, into an address.

Not her grandmother's handwriting. Sharper. Older.

She recognized the street name. It was in this town, maybe fifteen minutes away, out past the old mill.

She didn't think about it. She just got in the car.

The address led to a house she'd never seen before — narrow, sagging, ivy climbing one wall like it was trying to pull the building into the ground. No cars in the driveway. No lights on, despite the hour.

She almost turned back.

Instead she knocked.

It took a long time for the door to open. When it did, an old man stood in the frame, thin as a coat hanger, eyes pale and unfocused until they landed on her face — and then they sharpened all at once.

"You're back," he said.

"I've never been here before in my life."

He studied her for a long moment, head tilted, the way you'd look at a familiar word that had suddenly stopped looking like a word at all.

"No," he said slowly. "I suppose you haven't. Not this time."

"What does that mean?"

"You look like her," he said. "Almost exactly. But your eyes are wrong." He said it simply, without unkindness, the way you'd note the weather. "Come in, Rani."

"My name is Naina."

Something crossed his face — not quite surprise. Something closer to disappointment.

"Of course it is," he said. "This time."

He let her in anyway.

The inside of the house was exactly as sparse as the outside promised — a single armchair, a cold fireplace, and on every wall, photographs. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

Naina walked the length of the room, her stomach tightening with every step.

Different faces. Different decades, judging by the clothes and the grain of the film. But the same composition, again and again — a child, a garden, a birthday, a doorway. And in the corner of nearly every one, that same faceless shape, closer in some photographs than others.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"I keep the accounts," the old man said, settling into the armchair as though this were a conversation he'd had many times before, with many different people who looked almost exactly like her.

"What accounts?"

"Your grandmother came to me sixty years ago with a dying child in her arms." He said it plainly, the way you'd recite something memorized long ago. "You. Not two years old, and already gone, by every measure the doctors had. She wasn't willing to accept that."

Naina's mouth had gone dry.

"She made an arrangement," the old man continued. "For every year you lived, one memory would be owed. Not taken from you — she wouldn't allow that. Taken from *her* instead. Given up, and given to me, to keep the balance."

"That's not possible."

"She paid every year for sixty years," he said, as if she hadn't spoken. "She forgot her own wedding day. She forgot the sound of her mother's voice. Near the end, she was forgetting your birthdays, one detail at a time, and she never told you why, because she'd also agreed never to tell you why."

He looked at her, and for the first time, something like sympathy moved behind his pale eyes.

"When she died," he said, "the debt didn't end. It simply had no one left to collect it from but you."

Naina thought of the album. Of page four, page five, page six. Of a woman in a garden that no longer existed, replacing a memory Naina had never known was missing.

"The photographs," she whispered.

"Are what's left when a memory is taken," he said. "A record. Proof of what used to be true, before it wasn't anymore."

"And page seven?"

The old man's expression didn't change, but something in the room seemed to grow colder.

"Page seven," he said, "is not a memory from your past, Naina. There hasn't been time to owe one yet, not since your grandmother passed."

"Then what is it?"

"It's tonight," he said. "It's whichever memory you're holding most tightly to, right now, in this room, while we speak. The debt doesn't wait for old age anymore. There's no one left standing between you and it."

Naina thought, helplessly, of her mother's voice on the phone. *I'm coming to get you.*

She thought of the sound of it — worried, familiar, hers.

The old man was watching her face very carefully.

"I'd go home, if I were you," he said quietly. "Before midnight."
