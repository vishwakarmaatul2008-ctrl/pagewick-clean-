# Chapter 2 — What the Candles Remember

In the morning, Naina told herself she had imagined it.

Exhaustion. Grief. A strange house. It was an easy story to believe in daylight.

She made coffee in her grandmother's ancient kettle and sat at the kitchen table, determined not to think about the album again.

She lasted eleven minutes.

She went back into the living room and opened it.

Page one. Page two. Page three. Page four — the doorway, the shadow, exactly as she remembered.

She turned to page five.

It was empty.

Naina let out a breath she hadn't realized she was holding.

*See,* she told herself. *Nothing. You imagined the rest.*

She closed the album and spent the day packing boxes, trying to lose herself in the physical work of dismantling a life.

That evening, her mother called.

"How's the apartment?"

"Full of boxes," Naina said. "I found her old photo album. There are pictures of me I don't remember. One from my birthday — I think I was seven. Do you remember a party at Grandma's place?"

There was a pause on the line.

"What kind of party?"

"Cake, candles. There might've been someone else there. A man, maybe. Tall."

The pause this time was longer.

"There was no man," her mother said. Her voice had changed — careful now, too even. "It was just us. Me, you, and your grandmother. No one else was ever there."

"Are you sure?"

"Naina, I was there. I think I'd remember."

They talked a little longer, about nothing, and then hung up.

Naina sat with her phone in her hand for a long time.

She didn't believe her mother.

She wasn't sure why.

That night she went to bed early, determined to stay asleep through until morning.

She woke at 3 a.m. anyway.

She didn't bother pretending she wasn't going to check.

Page five was no longer empty.

The photograph showed her at the birthday party — seven years old, candlelight flickering across her face, mid-laugh, caught in a moment of pure unguarded joy.

Behind her, at the edge of the table, stood the same shape.

Taller this time. Closer.

Still no face. Just an absence shaped like a man.

Naina's hand trembled as she held the photograph closer.

There was something in his hand.

A small rectangle. Pale. She squinted.

It looked like another photograph.

As if the shape in the picture was holding a photograph of its own.

Naina slammed the album shut and didn't open it again that night.

The next morning, she called the one person from her childhood who might actually remember that birthday — her grandmother's old neighbor, Mrs. Dutta, who had lived two doors down for as long as anyone could remember and still did.

Mrs. Dutta answered on the third ring, delighted to hear from her, and invited her over for tea within the hour.

Naina brought the photograph.

Mrs. Dutta studied it for a long moment, her expression sliding from polite interest into something else.

"I don't remember this," she said slowly.

"You were there. I think. Weren't you?"

"I was there for your seventh birthday, yes. I remember the cake. I remember your grandmother's dress — the blue one, with the little flowers."

She looked up at Naina.

"But there was no man standing at that table. I would remember a stranger at a child's birthday party. I don't forget things like that."

"Then who is this?" Naina asked, tapping the shape at the edge of the photo.

Mrs. Dutta stared at it for a long moment.

"I don't know," she said finally. "But I don't like it."

She handed the photograph back like it had grown warm in her hands.

"Naina," she said. "Your grandmother used to say something, near the end. I thought it was just her mind going. Old age does strange things."

"What did she say?"

Mrs. Dutta hesitated.

"She said, *he only takes what's already been forgotten.*"

Naina felt something cold settle in her chest.

"What does that mean?"

Mrs. Dutta shook her head.

"I don't know. I never asked. I was afraid to."
