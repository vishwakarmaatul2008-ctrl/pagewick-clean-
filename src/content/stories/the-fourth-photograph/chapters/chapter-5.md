# Chapter 5 — What Her Grandmother Paid

Naina drove back too fast, headlights cutting through empty roads, the old man's words looping in her head.

*Whichever memory you're holding most tightly to.*

She didn't let herself finish the thought.

Her mother's car was already outside the apartment when she arrived — parked crooked, driver's door still open, like she'd abandoned it mid-motion.

Naina found her inside, standing over the coffee table, staring down at the album with her hand pressed against her mouth.

"Mum."

Her mother turned. Her eyes were red.

"I opened it," she said. "I shouldn't have. But I saw it sitting there and I — "

"Page seven?"

Her mother nodded, and stepped aside so Naina could see.

The page was no longer blank.

But it wasn't finished, either — the image was still resolving, ghostly and half-formed, the way a photograph looks in the seconds after the shutter clicks and before the picture knows what it's going to be.

Naina could make out shapes. A car. A road. Headlights.

Her own face, mid-motion, behind a steering wheel.

Tonight. This drive. Happening right now, being recorded into permanence even as it happened.

"What is this?" her mother whispered. "What is happening to your grandmother's album?"

Naina told her everything — the old man, the address, sixty years of a debt paid one forgotten memory at a time.

Her mother sat down heavily on the couch partway through, and by the time Naina finished, she was crying, silently, her hands pressed together in her lap.

"She never told me," her mother said. "All those years. I thought she was just getting old. Forgetting things. I used to get so frustrated with her — " She stopped, pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"She forgot her own wedding," Naina said quietly. "He told me that."

Her mother nodded, not trusting her voice.

"There's more," Naina said. "He said the debt doesn't wait anymore. That it's not going to take something from the past tonight." She looked at the half-formed photograph on page seven — herself, driving, right now, this very memory being pulled out from under her even as they spoke. "It's taking something happening right now. Whatever I'm holding onto the tightest."

Her mother's eyes went to the photograph. To her own daughter's face in it.

"Then it's me," she said, very quietly. "It's going to take me. This. Tonight. Us, together."

"I don't know."

"Naina." Her mother took her hands, gripped them hard enough to hurt. "Whatever happens, I need you to remember something before it takes it from you. I need you to *know* it, so deep down that even losing the memory of tonight can't touch it."

"Mum — "

"I love you," her mother said. "I have loved you every single day since the moment you were born, and I would have made the exact same deal your grandmother made, without a second's hesitation, if it meant you got to live. Do you understand me? Whatever this thing takes from you tonight — that part stays. I need you to promise me that part stays."

Naina couldn't speak. She just nodded, tears sliding down her face, and held onto her mother's hands as tightly as she could.

On the coffee table, page seven continued to resolve, line by line, the two of them coming into focus inside the frame — sitting exactly as they were sitting now, hands clasped, faces close.

A record of the last moment either of them might remember together.

Naina looked past the album, toward the window, where the streetlight outside had begun, impossibly, to dim — as if something large and quiet had stepped between it and the glass.
