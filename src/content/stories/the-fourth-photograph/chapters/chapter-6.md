# Chapter 6 — The Collector's Offer

The lights didn't go out completely. They simply thinned, the way daylight thins before a storm, until the apartment felt like it existed inside a held breath.

Naina's mother gripped her hand tighter.

"Don't let go," she whispered.

The shape came through the wall the way cold air comes through a gap under a door — not dramatically, just suddenly present where it hadn't been a moment before, standing in the corner of the living room where the shadow always stood in the photographs.

Up close, it wasn't quite faceless. There was the suggestion of features, the way a reflection in dark water suggests a face without ever quite showing one.

"You came home," the shape said. Its voice was unhurried. Almost gentle. "Most of them run."

"What do you want?" Naina asked.

"I don't want anything," it said. "I balance what's owed. Your grandmother borrowed sixty years against a debt she never intended for you to inherit. But debts don't simply end because the borrower does."

"Then let it fall on me the way it fell on her," Naina said. "One memory a year. I'll pay it the way she did."

The shape tilted its head, an oddly human gesture.

"She offered you that mercy because she loved you enough not to let you feel it happening," it said. "But she also spent sixty years paying alone, telling no one, watching herself disappear one memory at a time until she barely recognized her own history by the end. Is that truly the offer you want to make?"

Naina's mother made a small, broken sound beside her.

"There's another way," the shape continued. "The debt can end tonight. Completely. But it requires a single, larger payment, rather than sixty small ones."

"What payment?"

"The memory you're both holding onto right now," it said, its not-quite-eyes moving to their joined hands. "Not a childhood photograph. Not a stranger's face in a garden. This. Tonight. Everything either of you feels for the other, in this exact moment, given freely, all at once."

"You want us to forget each other," Naina's mother said, horrified.

"Not each other," the shape corrected. "Only this. Only tonight. You would still be mother and daughter tomorrow. You would simply never remember the night you sat here, holding hands, waiting for me."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then the debt continues as it always has," the shape said. "Small pieces, taken slowly, for as long as Naina lives. She will forget her graduation. She will forget the sound of a friend's laugh. She will forget things so small she won't even notice they're gone, until one day she looks at her own life and wonders why so much of it feels like someone else's."

Naina looked at her mother.

Her mother looked back, eyes wet, and Naina understood, with sudden and complete certainty, what her grandmother must have felt sixty years ago, standing in this same impossible position, holding a dying child, being asked to choose a shape of loss rather than whether to lose at all.

"There has to be another option," Naina said.

The shape considered her for a long moment.

"There is one," it said slowly, "though I am obligated to tell you it is rarely chosen."

"Tell me."

"You may take the debt onto yourself entirely — not spread across a lifetime, but assumed all at once, tonight, by you alone. In exchange, your mother remembers everything. Tonight remains hers, whole and untouched."

"And what would it cost me?"

"Everything currently in that album," the shape said. "Every photograph, every memory it represents — the birthday, the garden, the woman you never knew you'd lost. And this conversation, this room, this choice. All of it. Gone from you completely, tonight, all at once, rather than slowly, across sixty years."

Naina's mother started to protest — "No, absolutely not, she is not — "

"I'll do it," Naina said.
