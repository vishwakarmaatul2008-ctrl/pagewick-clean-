# Chapter 1 — The Plague of Teeth

The infestation did not begin in the granaries. It began in the nurseries, always after the lamps were doused.

In the autumn of 1376, the citizens of Hamelin noticed that the night air felt heavier, colder, and wet with the stench of stagnant river silt. With the dark came the sound—not the soft scurry of field mice, but a relentless, rhythmic scraping. Thousands of tiny, needle-sharp teeth grinding against timber, stone, and iron.

They were not ordinary rats. They operated strictly between dusk and dawn, moving in synchronized, shadow-like drifts through the unlit alleys. They ignored the grain stores and salt-cured meats. They craved lime, marrow, and bone.

On a Tuesday night, Mayor Hunke woke to an icy weight on his chest. In the pitch-black room, three massive rodents crouched upon his sternum. They were not biting his skin; they were chewing his brass nightshirt buttons down to metallic dust, their long yellow incisors grinding in terrifying unison. When he struck a match, the sudden flare caught a tall, impossibly thin shadow standing silently in the corner of his bedchamber—a silhouette wearing a deep hood with a sharp, unnaturally long nose protruding from the darkness before it vanished into the wall.

By December, the town was unraveling. Infants woke with the tips of their fingernails gnawed flat to the quick while they slept. The church bells rang at midnight not for prayer, but to gather men with iron spikes to clear the town wells, where hundreds of bloated, drowned rats formed a squirming plug deep in the dark.

Desperate, the Town Council emptied the public treasury to hire rat-catchers from across Lower Saxony. None survived three nights. One hunter was found sitting upright inside the eastern watchtower at dawn, his eyes wide with horror, his mouth crammed full of dried rat tails, his throat severed from the inside out.

On the forty-second night of the siege, as the Council sat in the candlelit Great Hall, the heavy oak portal slid open without a sound.

Framed against the pitch-black courtyard was a tall, unnaturally slender figure wrapped in a dark, trailing cloak stitched together with silver wire. His deep hood was pulled so far forward that his face was swallowed in absolute shadow—save for the sharp, terrifyingly elongated silhouette of a long, thin nose and the occasional glint of wet, pale teeth beneath his hidden lip.

Around his neck hung a narrow cylinder of yellowed, hollowed-out bone, bound in tarnished brass straps.

"Your streets are foul," a voice rasped from the dark recess of the hood. It sounded like dry riverbed stones grinding together deep under the earth. "Your children cry in their sleep because the floors move beneath their beds."

Mayor Hunke gripped his wooden gavel, his hand trembling violently. "Who gave you entry? The gates are locked at dusk."

"Locks keep out things that walk by day," the shadowed figure whispered. "I arrive when the debt of a city reaches its lip."

A long, spider-like finger emerged from the dark sleeve, pointing toward the window.

"One thousand gold guilders," the Piper said. "Pay me that sum when the rats are gone, and I will draw every sharp tooth out of Hamelin. Refuse me, or fail to pay, and the dark will balance its own ledger."
