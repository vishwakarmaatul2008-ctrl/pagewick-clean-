# Chapter 2 — The Song Beneath the Cobblestones

Mayor Hunke signed the parchment seal with a shaking quill. The hooded figure did not touch the parchment. He merely turned, his long nose cutting through the candlelight, and melted back into the night.

At precisely midnight, the Piper stood atop the stone rim of the central market square fountain. The moon was choked by thick black clouds. He pulled his hood even lower, concealing his entire face in shadow, and brought the yellowed bone pipe to his hidden lips.

He did not blow a melody. What came out was a low, vibrating hum—a frequency so deep it could not be heard through the ears, but was felt directly inside the marrow of the human jawbone.

Underneath the stone floors of every house, inside the cathedral walls, and beneath the cobbled streets, the scraping began.

The floorboards in the Great Hall buckled upward. Long, black streams of fur poured out like pressurized oil, cascading down the stone steps into the dark streets.

The Piper began to walk toward the North Gate. He moved with slow, ritualistic precision, his face completely buried beneath his cowl. Behind him followed a living, churning sea of black bodies.

The rats were not running out of fear; they were entranced. They filled the alleyways from wall to wall, their black backs glistening in the damp night, climbing over one another in a frantic, suffocating mass.

People watched from their shuttered second-story windows in paralyzed terror. A woman dropped an oil lamp into the street; the glass shattered on the backs of a hundred moving rodents, but they did not scatter from the flames. They marched through the fire, their burning bodies trailing smoke, completely indifferent to the pain as long as they followed that low, bone-grinding hum from the hidden mouth beneath the hood.

The Piper reached the black, turbulent waters of the Weser River. Without slowing his pace, he stepped off the stone embankment and walked directly onto the surface of the pitch-black water. He did not sink. He stood upon the river like solid glass, his shadowy form blending seamlessly into the night.

Behind him, the river of vermin plunged off the stone wall into the icy current, drowning by the tens of thousands until the water was black with squirming fur.

By 3:00 AM, the last rat was gone. Silence descended on Hamelin—a heavy, suffocating silence that felt darker than the infestation itself.
