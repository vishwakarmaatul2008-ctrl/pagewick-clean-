# Chapter 2: Before Sunrise

The first light of dawn crept over Mumbai, revealing a city that no longer belonged to the living.

Aarav stood beside the apartment door, tightening the straps of his backpack. Inside it were two bottles of water, a few biscuits, a small flashlight, and an iron pipe he had taken from the building's storage room.

It wasn't much.

But it was everything he could afford to carry.

“Tell me again why we're trusting a stranger on the radio,” Kabir whispered.

“Because the official channel wants us to go to a collection point that someone has already warned us about,” Aarav replied.

“And the stranger wants us to go to a railway yard,” Kabir said. “Which could also be a trap.”

“Exactly.”

Kabir stared at him.

Aarav sighed. “I didn't say it was a good plan.”

“Fantastic. That's reassuring.”

Under different circumstances, Aarav might have laughed.

Now he barely had the energy.

Across the room, Dev was checking the old map Rishi had drawn. The railway maintenance yard lay west of their current position, beyond a narrow service road that ran behind several commercial buildings.

The route wasn't long.

The problem was everything between here and there.

“There's a main road crossing,” Rishi said, pointing at the map. “We avoid that. Too many abandoned cars, and we saw infected near the junction yesterday.”

“We could cut through the residential lanes,” Meera suggested.

“Only if the gates are open,” Dev replied. “Some of those buildings have locked entrances. If we get trapped in a lane, we're finished.”

Sana adjusted the straps on her backpack.

“What about Nandini and Arjun?”

The room fell quiet.

Nandini was awake, but barely. Her fever had left her exhausted, and she struggled to sit upright without Priya's help.

Arjun sat beside her, holding a small cloth bag containing the few belongings his mother had managed to collect. He hadn't asked about his father again.

That silence worried Sana more than his questions had.

“We can't leave them here,” Priya said firmly.

“Nobody suggested that,” Aarav answered.

“But she can't walk far,” Priya continued. “And if we have to run…”

She couldn't finish the sentence.

Aarav looked at Nandini.

For the past several hours, he had tried to avoid thinking about her condition. There was no medicine, no doctor, and no reliable information about how long a bitten person remained themselves.

He had seen what happened to Vikram.

He had watched Nandini's fear whenever her fever rose.

He knew they couldn't pretend the problem would disappear.

“We'll make the journey in stages,” he said. “We'll stop somewhere safe if we can. Nobody gets left behind.”

Nandini opened her eyes.

“You shouldn't risk everyone for me.”

Aarav turned toward her.

“We're not leaving you.”

“You don't understand.” Her voice trembled. “If I change while we're outside…”

“We'll deal with that if it happens.”

“No.” Nandini shook her head. “You need to promise me something.”

Aarav didn't answer.

“Promise me that if I become dangerous, you won't let me hurt Arjun.”

Arjun looked up.

“Mom?”

Nandini immediately forced a smile.

“It's nothing, sweetheart.”

But he had heard enough to understand that something was wrong.

He crawled closer and wrapped both arms around her.

Aarav looked away.

He had promised to protect Arjun, but the promise felt heavier every time someone repeated it.

Finally, he nodded.

“I promise we'll protect him.”

Nandini studied his face.

It wasn't exactly the answer she wanted.

But it was the only honest one he could give.

---

At 5:32 a.m., they left the apartment.

Sana stayed close to Arjun, holding his hand. Priya supported Nandini on one side while Meera took the other. Dev carried the map and flashlight, Rishi carried the radio, and Kabir walked ahead with Aarav.

They moved quietly down the stairwell.

Every step seemed impossibly loud.

At the ground floor, Aarav stopped and listened.

Nothing.

He leaned toward the narrow glass panel beside the entrance.

The street looked empty.

An abandoned scooter lay on its side near the pavement. A bus blocked part of the intersection, its windows shattered. Farther away, smoke rose behind a row of buildings.

But there were no visible infected.

“Clear,” Aarav whispered.

Kabir eased the door open.

They stepped outside.

The air smelled of smoke, damp concrete, and something rotten.

Mumbai was usually awake by this hour. Tea stalls opened before sunrise. Newspaper vendors moved through residential streets. Delivery vehicles filled the roads, and the first local trains carried people toward work.

Now there were no vendors.

No trains they could hear.

No ordinary morning sounds.

Only the wind pushing scraps of paper along the pavement.

They moved toward the western service road.

After several minutes, Dev raised his hand.

Everyone stopped.

“What?” Meera whispered.

Dev pointed toward the next junction.

A military vehicle stood beside a barricade.

Two soldiers were visible near the front. Another stood beside a portable floodlight, even though the sky was beginning to brighten.

A line of civilians waited behind the barricade.

Some carried suitcases. Others held children or supported injured relatives. A few had blankets wrapped around their shoulders.

They looked exhausted.

But they were alive.

For a moment, hope rose in Aarav's chest.

Then he noticed the white bands around their wrists.

Every person in the line wore one.

A soldier checked each band before allowing someone through the barricade. Those who passed were directed toward a large transport truck parked farther down the road.

A woman suddenly stepped out of the line.

“I told you, my husband is still inside!” she shouted. “Please, I can't leave him there!”

A soldier grabbed her arm.

“Return to the line.”

“He's injured! He can't walk!”

“Return to the line.”

The woman pulled away.

Another soldier approached, holding a small device. He scanned the white band around her wrist.

A light flashed.

The soldier looked at his colleague.

“She's not cleared.”

“What does that mean?” the woman demanded.

Neither soldier answered.

Two men took her by the arms and led her toward a separate vehicle.

She began screaming.

Nobody in the line moved to help her.

Nobody even looked at her for long.

They were too frightened of becoming the next person pulled away.

Aarav felt Kabir's hand close around his shoulder.

“Don't,” Kabir whispered.

Aarav hadn't realized he was moving forward.

“We have to help her.”

“With what?” Kabir hissed. “We don't know what's happening, and we can't fight an entire military unit.”

The woman disappeared behind the vehicle.

A door slammed.

The truck's engine started.

Then the transport vehicle pulled away, carrying the people who had passed inspection.

Aarav watched it disappear around the corner.

“That's not a rescue,” Meera whispered.

“No,” Rishi replied. “It's a selection process.”

They backed away from the junction and slipped behind an abandoned delivery van.

For several seconds, nobody spoke.

Then the radio crackled inside Rishi's backpack.

Everyone froze.

He pulled it out and lowered the volume.

A voice emerged through the static.

“Collection point west sector. Transport unit departing. Twelve accepted. One rejected. Awaiting further instructions.”

A pause followed.

Then another voice answered.

“Maintain screening. Priority subjects must be recovered before the next transfer.”

Rishi slowly turned the radio off.

“Priority subjects,” Dev repeated. “That's what the man called us.”

Aarav stared at the road where the truck had vanished.

They had been right to distrust the official message.

But now they had another problem.

If the military was screening survivors and transporting selected people away, what happened to those who failed?

And why were six ordinary friends on a list of people who had to be recovered?

---

They continued along the service road, keeping the buildings between themselves and the checkpoint.

Nandini's breathing grew heavier.

Priya stopped beside a shuttered pharmacy and helped her sit on a low concrete step.

“We need to rest,” Priya said.

“We can't stay here,” Kabir replied, looking anxiously down the street.

“She's going to collapse if we keep moving.”

Kabir looked at Nandini.

His expression softened, but his eyes kept scanning the road.

“Two minutes,” he said. “Then we move.”

Sana gave Arjun a biscuit.

He took it but didn't eat.

“Are the soldiers bad people?” he asked.

Sana crouched beside him.

“I don't know.”

“Then why are we hiding from them?”

She struggled to find an answer that a six-year-old could understand.

“Sometimes people do things we don't understand. We need to be careful until we know whether they're safe.”

Arjun looked toward the checkpoint.

“Will they take my mom?”

Sana's throat tightened.

“No one is taking your mom.”

She hoped it was true.

Across the road, a shutter moved.

Aarav noticed it immediately.

He raised his hand, signalling everyone to remain still.

A man peered through a narrow opening in the metal shutter. He was middle-aged, with a thick beard and a bandage around his forehead.

When he saw the group, he opened the shutter just enough to reveal his face.

“You people want to stay alive?” he whispered.

Aarav stepped forward cautiously.

“Who are you?”

“Someone who made the mistake of trusting that checkpoint.”

The man looked toward Nandini.

Then his eyes settled on Arjun.

“Get inside. Quickly.”

Kabir raised the iron pipe.

“Why should we trust you?”

“You shouldn't,” the man replied. “But if you stand in the middle of this road much longer, someone will notice you.”

He disappeared inside.

The shutter remained partly open.

Aarav looked at his friends.

It was another stranger offering help.

Another door that might lead to safety.

Or another trap.

“We don't have time,” Dev whispered.

Aarav made his decision.

“One at a time. Weapons ready. If anything looks wrong, we get out.”

Kabir went first.

Then Meera, Dev, and Rishi followed. Sana helped Arjun through the opening while Priya and Aarav supported Nandini.

The man pulled the shutter closed behind them.

Inside, the shop smelled of dust and old medicine. Shelves stood half-empty, and several boxes had been pushed against the rear door.

The man pointed toward the back.

“Keep your voices down.”

“Who are you?” Aarav demanded again.

The man ignored the question.

Instead, he stared at the radio in Rishi's hand.

“Where did you get that?”

“Municipal office.”

The man's expression changed.

“Did you hear the warehouse broadcast?”

Nobody answered.

He looked from one face to another.

Then he noticed Nandini's feverish eyes and Arjun standing beside her.

His shoulders sank.

“Damn it,” he whispered.

Aarav tightened his grip on the iron pipe.

“What?”

The man moved toward a small table and pulled out a folded sheet of paper.

It was a list.

Several names had been written in black ink.

Some were crossed out.

Others had numbers beside them.

Aarav couldn't see the entire page, but he recognized one name near the bottom.

PRIYA.

Priya stepped forward.

“Where did you get that?”

The man looked at her.

“Your brother gave it to me.”

For a moment, the shop seemed to stop breathing.

“My brother?” Priya whispered.

“He was working near the eastern industrial zone. He found out what was happening at the warehouse and tried to get information out.”

“Where is he now?”

The man lowered his eyes.

“I don't know.”

Priya grabbed the edge of the table.

“Is he alive?”

“I said I don't know.”

“Then why is my name on that list?”

The man finally looked up.

“Because your brother believed you might come looking for him.”

Priya's face went pale.

Aarav stepped closer to the table.

“What's the list for?”

The man hesitated.

Then he turned the paper around.

At the top, a heading had been written in capital letters:

RECOVERY PRIORITY — EASTERN SECTOR

Below it were several names.

Aarav read the first one.

Then the second.

His stomach tightened.

The list contained the names of people they had heard over the radio, people who had disappeared from the streets, and survivors who had reportedly been taken to the warehouse.

Near the bottom were six names he knew.

Aarav.

Kabir.

Dev.

Rishi.

Meera.

Sana.

All six friends.

Beside their names was a single word.

UNCONFIRMED.

Nobody spoke.

Then the man folded the paper and slipped it into Aarav's hand.

“Now you understand why I told you to stay away from the collection point.”

Aarav stared at the list.

“Who made this?”

The man looked toward the shutter.

Outside, an engine slowed near the pharmacy.

A vehicle door opened.

Footsteps approached.

The man went completely still.

Then he whispered the words that made Aarav's blood run cold.

“The people looking for you have arrived.”

A fist struck the shutter.

Once.

Twice.

Then a voice called from the other side.

“We know you're in there.”

Aarav raised the iron pipe.

Behind him, Nandini struggled to remain standing, Priya clutched the table, and Arjun buried his face against Sana.

The voice outside spoke again.

“Open the door. We're here to take you somewhere safe.”

Aarav looked at the list in his hand.

Six names.

One word.

UNCONFIRMED.

He finally understood the danger.

They hadn't stumbled into the middle of someone else's operation.

They had been part of it from the beginning.

And whatever had happened at Phoenix Mall, someone had decided that the six friends were too important to leave behind.

The fist struck the shutter again.

Harder this time.

“Open the door,” the voice repeated.

Aarav looked at his friends.

Nobody moved.

Then the man beside the table reached beneath the counter and pulled out a pistol.

He aimed it at the shutter.

“Whatever happens,” he whispered, “do not let them take the child.”

Outside, metal scraped against metal.

Someone was beginning to force the door open.

And dawn had only just begun.
