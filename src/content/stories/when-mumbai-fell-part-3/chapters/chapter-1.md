# Chapter 1: The Place They Called Safety

The radio crackled at 3:17 a.m.

Nobody moved.

Aarav sat beside the window, holding the radio in both hands. Outside, Mumbai was almost unrecognizable. The streets that had once been crowded with taxis, buses, and people rushing to work were now filled with abandoned vehicles and wandering infected.

Smoke rose from somewhere beyond the railway tracks.

Every few minutes, a distant scream broke through the silence.

Then the radio spoke again.

“—all remaining survivors are advised to proceed to the designated collection point near the eastern industrial zone. Transport will be provided. Repeat, transport will be provided.”

Kabir lifted his head from the floor.

“Did they just say eastern industrial zone?”

Aarav nodded.

Dev sat up immediately. “The same place that woman was talking about?”

Nobody answered.

They had all heard the previous transmissions. Survivors trapped inside a warehouse. Soldiers refusing to let people leave. Someone claiming that the people being taken there weren't being rescued.

And now the emergency channel was directing survivors straight toward it.

“Turn it off,” Sana whispered.

Aarav looked at her.

“Please. I can't listen to another message telling us we're safe when nobody is coming to help us.”

He lowered the volume, but didn't switch it off.

Across the room, Meera was sitting beside the apartment door, her knees pulled against her chest. Rishi stood near the kitchen, checking the remaining supplies for the third time that night.

Two water bottles.

Half a packet of biscuits.

Some instant noodles they couldn't cook without finding a reliable source of water and a safe place to prepare them.

That was everything they had.

And there were seven people in the apartment who needed to eat.

Actually, eight.

Arjun was asleep beside his mother, his small hand wrapped around the sleeve of Nandini's shirt. Even in sleep, he seemed frightened. Every distant bang made him twitch.

Nandini lay on a mattress near the wall.

Her fever had worsened.

The skin around her eyes looked pale, and sweat covered her forehead despite the cool air coming through the broken window. Priya had been sitting beside her for hours, changing the cloth on her forehead and checking her breathing.

Aarav watched them from across the room.

He couldn't stop thinking about what Nandini had said.

*If I stop recognizing him, promise me you won't let me hurt my son.*

He had promised.

But he had no idea whether he could keep that promise.

“Guys,” Priya whispered.

Everyone turned toward her.

Nandini's eyes were open.

She was staring at the ceiling, breathing slowly.

“Is it morning?” she asked.

Priya leaned closer. “Not yet. You should rest.”

Nandini turned her head toward Arjun.

“Has he eaten?”

Priya hesitated.

“A little.”

Nandini understood the hesitation.

“Give him whatever is left.”

“We're saving it for everyone,” Priya said gently.

“He's six.”

Her voice was weak, but the words carried a mother's determination that the outbreak hadn't managed to destroy.

Priya looked down.

Then she broke the remaining biscuit into smaller pieces and placed them beside Arjun.

Nandini watched her.

“Thank you.”

Priya shook her head, unable to answer.

For a moment, the room felt almost normal.

A mother worrying about her child.

Friends sharing food.

People trying to survive another morning.

Then Nandini suddenly grabbed Priya's wrist.

“Listen to me.”

Priya leaned forward.

“If I change…”

“Don't.”

“Please.” Nandini's fingers tightened. “Don't let him see me like that. Take him away before it happens.”

Priya's eyes filled with tears.

“I promise.”

Nandini released her wrist.

Then she closed her eyes.

Aarav looked away.

He had seen fear in the mall. He had seen people run, scream, and abandon strangers to save themselves.

But watching someone prepare to lose her own life while worrying about her child was different.

It made the entire disaster feel crueler than any infected creature could ever be.

---

At 4:02 a.m., the radio changed.

A different voice came through.

This one was calmer.

Official.

“Attention, survivors. A temporary medical screening and evacuation point is now operational at the eastern perimeter. Individuals who arrive before sunrise will be registered for transport. Bring identification if available. Do not approach the main warehouse entrance.”

Dev frowned.

“Why would they tell people not to approach the main entrance?”

“Maybe there's another entrance,” Rishi suggested.

“Or maybe they don't want people seeing what's happening inside,” Meera replied.

Kabir rubbed his face.

“Okay. Let's say the transmission is real. We go there, find out what's happening, and get out. Simple.”

“Nothing has been simple since Phoenix Mall,” Sana said.

Kabir didn't argue.

Aarav turned the radio off.

“We need to know whether that place is safe. But we're not taking everyone there blindly.”

“Then what do we do?” Dev asked.

Aarav glanced at Nandini.

They couldn't stay in the apartment forever. The supplies were running out, and they had no guarantee that the infected wouldn't find their hiding place.

But moving Nandini through the city could be dangerous.

And taking Arjun toward an unknown military operation might be even worse.

“We scout first,” Aarav said. “A small group goes near the collection point. We observe, gather information, and return. Nobody approaches the warehouse itself.”

“I'll go,” Kabir said.

“So will I,” Dev added.

Rishi looked toward the window. “We need someone who can read a map. If the roads are blocked, we need another route.”

“I'm coming,” Meera said.

Sana shook her head. “If all of you go, who stays here?”

Silence followed.

Finally, Sana volunteered to remain with Priya, Nandini, and Arjun.

Aarav didn't like the idea of leaving anyone behind, but someone had to protect the people who couldn't travel.

Before they could finalize the plan, a faint sound came from outside.

A metallic click.

Everyone froze.

It came again.

Click.

Click.

Like someone tapping a tool against the apartment building's gate.

Rishi slowly moved toward the window.

“Don't,” Meera whispered.

But he was already looking through the gap in the curtains.

The street below was empty.

At first.

Then a figure stepped out from behind an abandoned car.

A man.

He wore a dark jacket and carried a backpack. His clothes were dirty, but he wasn't stumbling like the infected. He moved carefully, looking at the buildings as if searching for something.

He stopped beneath their window.

Rishi held his breath.

The stranger looked up.

For one terrifying second, Rishi thought their eyes had met.

Then the man raised a small radio to his mouth.

“I'm at the residential block,” he whispered. “The building matches the description.”

Rishi backed away from the window.

His heart hammered against his ribs.

The stranger listened to a reply.

Then he spoke again.

“Yes. I can confirm the group is still here.”

Rishi turned toward Aarav.

He didn't need to say anything.

Aarav understood immediately.

Someone knew where they were.

Someone had been following them.

The man outside lowered his radio and walked toward the neighbouring building.

He didn't try to enter.

He didn't call out.

He simply disappeared around the corner.

Kabir rushed to the window but stopped before exposing himself.

“Was that one of the soldiers?”

“I don't know,” Rishi replied. “But he was talking about us.”

“Maybe he saw us near the municipal office,” Dev said.

“Or at the grocery store,” Meera added.

Aarav stared at the street.

The mysterious rooftop watcher from earlier had reported six survivors moving west. They had assumed the danger was limited to someone observing them from a distance.

Now someone had found their shelter.

And the stranger hadn't looked surprised to find them.

He had looked as though he had finally reached the correct address.

---

By 4:30 a.m., nobody was thinking about sleep.

Aarav moved the remaining furniture against the apartment door. Dev checked the windows. Kabir searched the adjoining rooms for another exit, while Rishi drew a rough map of the surrounding streets on the back of an old newspaper.

Meera stayed close to Arjun.

Sana sat beside Nandini, holding her hand.

Priya stood near the doorway, listening for footsteps in the corridor.

Then Arjun woke up.

“Where's my dad?”

Nobody answered immediately.

He looked at their faces, one by one.

“Is he coming back?”

Sana knelt beside him.

“Arjun…”

“Mom said Dad was sick.” His voice trembled. “She said he needed to stay in the other room. But then he started hitting the door.”

Sana swallowed.

Arjun looked toward Nandini.

“Is he still angry with me?”

“No,” Sana whispered, pulling him into a hug. “None of this is your fault.”

The boy buried his face against her shoulder.

Across the room, Nandini began to cry silently.

Aarav turned away, unable to watch.

He had spent the last few days thinking survival meant finding food, avoiding infected people, and reaching somewhere safe.

Now he was beginning to understand something else.

Survival meant carrying the memories of everyone they couldn't save.

And sometimes, it meant making decisions that would haunt them long after the danger had passed.

---

At 5:06 a.m., the radio switched on by itself.

Aarav spun around.

The device sat on the floor where he had left it.

Static filled the room.

Then a voice spoke.

Not the official emergency broadcast.

Not the frightened woman from the warehouse.

A new voice.

A man's voice, low and urgent.

“If the six survivors from the Phoenix Mall incident can hear this, listen carefully.”

Aarav felt the blood drain from his face.

Kabir stared at him.

The voice continued.

“You are being directed toward a collection point that does not exist on the official evacuation map. Do not trust the transport vehicles. Do not allow anyone to separate you from the others.”

A burst of static swallowed the next few words.

Then the transmission returned.

“They know about the woman and the child. They know one of you has been listening to the warehouse broadcasts.”

Priya slowly stood up.

“How could they know about us?”

Nobody answered.

The voice became even quieter.

“If you want to survive, reach the old railway maintenance yard before sunrise. Come through the western service road. Bring no military escort. Trust no one wearing a white identification band.”

Aarav grabbed the radio.

“Who are you?” he shouted. “How do you know our names?”

For several seconds, there was only static.

Then the man answered.

“I know because I was there when your names were added to the list.”

Aarav's grip tightened.

“What list?”

The reply came in a whisper.

“The list of survivors who must be recovered.”

A loud crack exploded through the radio.

The transmission died.

Everyone stared at the silent device.

Outside, an engine started.

Aarav rushed to the window just in time to see a dark van turn onto the road. Its rear windows were covered, and a small white band had been tied around the driver's wrist.

The same kind of band the mysterious voice had warned them about.

The van slowed beside the building.

For a moment, Aarav could see the driver's face through the windscreen.

The man looked directly toward their window.

Then he smiled.

The vehicle continued down the street.

Aarav stepped away from the glass.

They had two choices now.

Follow the official instructions toward the eastern collection point, where someone might be waiting to capture them.

Or trust an anonymous voice and head for an abandoned railway maintenance yard before sunrise.

Neither option promised safety.

Behind him, Nandini began coughing violently.

Priya rushed to her side.

Arjun started crying.

And somewhere outside, the first infected screams of the morning echoed through the streets of Mumbai.

Aarav looked at his friends.

“We leave before sunrise,” he said.

Kabir nodded. “For the railway yard?”

“For answers.”

Aarav glanced at the radio one last time.

“And if that voice is lying?”

He looked toward the door.

“Then we'll have to survive that, too.”

Outside, the sky began to lighten.

The city was waking up.

But for the six friends, the real nightmare was no longer hiding among the infected.

It was watching them from the shadows.

And it already knew their names.
